# Buddhist Representation and Factual Accuracy Review

**Author:** Manus AI  
**Review date:** August 25, 2026

The rebuilt IBAA public content was reviewed through seven independent editorial lenses: taxonomy, sectarian hierarchy, geographic balance, modern and diasporic communities, terminology and self-description, source discipline, and organizational launch readiness. The review compared the implemented copy with academic, museum, and tradition-authored sources that emphasize both Buddhism’s shared histories and its substantial regional, institutional, linguistic, and contemporary diversity.[1] [2] [3]

> **Editorial position:** IBAA’s Theravada–Mahayana–Vajrayana framework is useful for national cooperation, but it is not presented as a complete taxonomy, a set of sealed denominations, or a hierarchy.

## Representation Findings

The implemented traditions page avoids treating a three-part umbrella as a universal scholarly consensus. It explicitly explains that Vajrayana developed within wider Mahayana histories while also recognizing related esoteric traditions beyond Tibet.[1] [2] The East Asian section names Pure Land, Chan, Seon, Zen, Thiền, Tiantai, Tendai, Huayan, Kegon, Nichiren, and other contexts rather than using Zen as shorthand for all East Asian Buddhism. The South Asian section recognizes Newar Buddhism independently rather than treating it as a Tibetan subdivision, consistent with contemporary scholarship on Newar continuity and renewal.[5]

The practice framework includes ethics, generosity, study, teaching, devotion, ritual, meditation, pilgrimage, festivals, humanitarian service, peacebuilding, chaplaincy, ecological care, and responses to injustice. This prevents silent meditation or modernist interpretation from standing in for the whole of Buddhist religious life. Modern and reform movements are presented as plural and historically situated, including Humanistic Buddhism, Engaged Buddhism, Wŏn Buddhism, Ambedkarite or Navayana communities, Soka Gakkai, insight networks, and digital communities.[4] [6]

The diasporic section recognizes Asian and Asian American temples, immigrant and refugee communities, Black Buddhist communities, multiracial and mixed-heritage sanghas, chaplaincy, and transnational networks. These communities are not organized through an “ethnic versus convert” binary. Photography on the traditions page uses documented images from Thailand, Japan, and IBAA’s own United States archive, with creator, license, location, and provenance retained in the project research record.

## Corrections Applied After Independent Review

The Study Circle host record for **Won Buddhist Temple** now uses the directory label **Modern / reform** rather than automatically assigning it to Mahayana. The public directory explains that all tradition labels require confirmation by the community or an authorized IBAA roster; the temple’s public-facing spelling remains “Won” until the community confirms whether it prefers a diacritic in the proper name.

The About page now labels founding dates, names, incorporation milestones, and tax-status milestones as claims preserved from the legacy organizational history pending confirmation by current leadership. Study Circle resumption language similarly states that the **legacy archive records** the May 2024 date. The homepage no longer promises a monthly schedule and instead describes **recurring gatherings**.

## Editorial Recommendation

The worldwide Buddhist traditions content is suitable as a carefully sourced public foundation. It should still receive the planned IBAA monastic and lay adviser review because community self-description cannot be replaced by academic synthesis alone. Future editors should retain the existing non-ranking language, distinguish tradition-specific voices from umbrella summaries, avoid “Hinayana” as a label for living communities, and update named communities only from an authorized roster.[7] [8]
